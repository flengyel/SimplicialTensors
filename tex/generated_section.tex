\section{Generated submodules}
\label{sec:generated}
Let $K$ be a field, $n\geq0$ and $\mathbf c\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^k$.
Put $X=X(\mathbf c;K)$.
Let $P=K[\Delta^n]$ be the free simplicial $K$-module on the standard
$n$-simplex, so $P_p$ has basis the nondecreasing maps $[p]\to[n]$.
For $T\in X_n$, the representing property of $P$ gives
\[
 f_T:P_\bullet\longrightarrow X_\bullet,
 \qquad f_{T,p}(\alpha)=X(\alpha)(T)
 \quad\bigl(\alpha:[p]\to[n]\bigr).
\]
Its image is the simplicial submodule $\langle T\rangle$ generated by $T$.
For any simplicial module $Y$, the projection $\nu_p:Y_p\to N_p(Y)$ is
\[
 \nu_0=1,\qquad
 \nu_p=(1-s_0d_1)\cdots(1-s_{p-1}d_p)\quad(p>0).
\]
Apply the factors from right to left. Each $1-s_{i-1}d_i$ has image in
$\ker d_i$, preserves $\ker d_j$ for $j>i$, and induces the identity
modulo $D_p(Y)$. The splitting $Y_p=N_p(Y)\oplus D_p(Y)$ gives the formula.
In $P_p$ the noninjective maps span $D_p(P)$, so the $\nu_p\alpha$
for increasing injections $\alpha:[p]\hookrightarrow[n]$ form a basis of $N_p(P)$.
Since $f_T$ commutes with faces and degeneracies, it sends this basis
to the columns $\nu_p X(\alpha)(T)$ spanning $N_p(\langle T\rangle)$.
Let $r_p(T)$ be their rank. For $p>0$ let $\delta_p(T)$ be the rank of their
images under $d_0$, and put $\delta_0(T)=0$.
The $p$th Betti number is $r_p(T)-\delta_p(T)-\delta_{p+1}(T)$.
Only degrees $p\leq\min(n,k)$ enter the computation. For $p>n$ there are
no columns, and for $p>k$ the ambient $N_p(X)$ vanishes by
Theorem~\ref{thm:hornbasis}.

Put
\[
 C=N(P),\qquad g_T=N(f_T),\qquad L(T)=\ker g_T.
\]
Normalization is exact, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generated-normalization}
 N(\langle T\rangle)\cong C/L(T).
\end{equation}
Here $\dim C_p=\binom{n+1}{p+1}$ for $0\leq p\leq n$ and $C_p=0$ for $p>n$.
By Dold--Kan the subspaces $L_p(T)$ determine $\ker f_T$. For
$T,T'\in X_n$, $L(T)=L(T')$ holds precisely when there is a simplicial
isomorphism $\langle T\rangle\to\langle T'\rangle$ sending $T$ to $T'$.
In shape $(2,2)$ the generators $E_{01}$ and $E_{10}$ have the same
kernel ($L_0=C_0$, $L_1=0$) and span distinct lines in degree one.

Since $H_0(C)=K$ and
$H_j(C)=0$ for $j>0$, the short exact sequence
$0\to L(T)\to C\to C/L(T)\to0$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:generated-homology}
 \begin{gathered}
 H_j(\langle T\rangle)\cong H_{j-1}(L(T))\qquad(j\geq2),\\
 0\longrightarrow H_1(\langle T\rangle)\longrightarrow H_0(L(T))
 \xrightarrow{\epsilon}K\longrightarrow H_0(\langle T\rangle)
 \longrightarrow0,
 \end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ is induced by the augmentation sending every vertex of
$\Delta^n$ to $1$.

\subsection{Quotients and automorphisms}

We work in the category $\operatorname{Ch}_{\geq0}(K)$ of nonnegative
chain complexes of $K$-vector spaces and chain maps. A complex is finite
if the direct sum of its chain groups is finite-dimensional. For a complex $E$,
write
\[
 Z_p(E)=\ker(\partial_p:E_p\to E_{p-1}),\qquad
 B_p(E)=\im(\partial_{p+1}:E_{p+1}\to E_p),
 \qquad E_{-1}=0.
\]
Thus $Z_0(E)=E_0$. For a $K$-vector space $V$ and
$p\geq0$, let $S^p(V)$ be the complex with $V$ in degree $p$ and zero elsewhere. Choosing complements of boundaries
inside cycles, and of cycles inside each chain group, gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finite-chain-splitting}
 E\cong\bigoplus_{p\geq0}S^p(H_p(E))
       \oplus\bigoplus_{p\geq1}D^p(B_{p-1}(E))
\end{equation}
for every finite complex $E$. Indeed, the differential maps the chosen
complement of $Z_p(E)$ isomorphically onto $B_{p-1}(E)$.
Thus the homology dimensions and differential ranks determine the
isomorphism class of a finite complex. Their additivity gives cancellation
under direct sums.

For the normalized simplex complex, the dimensions of its chain groups
and its homology give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:simplex-projective}
 C\cong S^0(K)\oplus\bigoplus_{p=1}^n
                   D^p\bigl(K^{\binom np}\bigr).
\end{equation}
Chain maps from $S^0(V)$ and $D^p(V)$ to $E$ correspond to linear
maps $V\to E_0$ and $V\to E_p$, respectively. Evaluation in each
degree and $\operatorname{Hom}_K(V,-)$ are exact, so these summands
are projective and hence so is $C$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:quotient-lift}
Fix a field $K$ and $n\geq0$, and let $C=N(K[\Delta^n])$.
For subcomplexes $L,M\subseteq C$, every specified chain isomorphism
$\varphi:C/L\to C/M$ lifts to a chain automorphism $u:C\to C$
such that the square commutes.
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
 C \arrow[r,"u"] \arrow[d,"\pi_L"'] & C \arrow[d,"\pi_M"]\\
 C/L \arrow[r,"\varphi"'] & C/M .
\end{tikzcd}
\]
Here $\pi_L$ and $\pi_M$ are the quotient maps. In particular,
$C/L\cong C/M$ if and only if $u(L)=M$ for some
$u\in\operatorname{Aut}_{\operatorname{Ch}_{\geq0}(K)}(C)$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Projectivity supplies chain maps $f,g:C\to C$ with
$\pi_Mf=\varphi\pi_L$ and $\pi_Lg=\varphi^{-1}\pi_M$.
Put $h=gf$ and $h'=fg$. For sufficiently large $m$ their Fitting
decompositions give direct sums of subcomplexes
\[
 C=U\oplus V=\im h^m\oplus\ker h^m,\qquad
 C=U'\oplus V'=\im (h')^m\oplus\ker (h')^m.
\]
The identities $fh=h'f$ and $gh'=hg$ give $f(U)\subseteq U'$ and
$g(U')\subseteq U$. Since $gf$ is invertible on $U$, $f|_U$ is
injective. Since $U'=fg(U')\subseteq f(U)$, it maps onto $U'$.
Cancellation in \eqref{eq:finite-chain-splitting} gives
an isomorphism $a:V\to V'$. From $\pi_L(gf)^m=\pi_L$ and
$\pi_M(fg)^m=\pi_M$ we get $V\subseteq L$ and $V'\subseteq M$.
The automorphism $u=f|_U\oplus a$ then satisfies $\pi_Mu=\varphi\pi_L$
on $U$, by the choice of $f$, and on $V$, where both sides vanish.
Taking kernels gives $u(L)=M$.
Conversely, such an automorphism induces an isomorphism of quotients.
\end{proof}

Canfell's theorem on right equivalence of presentations
\cite[p.~141]{Canfell1992} also gives the lift. Complexes in degrees
$0,\ldots,n$ are modules over the path algebra of $n\to\cdots\to0$
modulo paths of length two. This algebra and its opposite are
finite-dimensional and hence semilocal of stable range one
\cite[Corollary~6.5]{Bass1964}. Thus Canfell's theorem applies to the
epimorphisms $\varphi\pi_L$ and $\pi_M$ from the finitely generated
projective module $C$.

For $a\in\operatorname{Aut}_{\operatorname{Ch}_{\geq0}(K)}(C)$,
let $\widetilde a:P\to P$ be its simplicial automorphism under Dold--Kan.
The tensor
\[
 T'=f_{T,n}\bigl(\widetilde a_n(\operatorname{id}_{[n]})\bigr)\in X_n
\]
satisfies $f_{T'}=f_T\widetilde a$ by the representing property of $P$.
Hence $L(T')=a^{-1}L(T)$ and
$\langle T'\rangle=\langle T\rangle$. Thus the set
$\{L(T):T\in X_n\}$ is invariant under the automorphism group.
Equation~\eqref{eq:generated-normalization}, Dold--Kan and
Proposition~\ref{prop:quotient-lift} identify its orbits with the
simplicial isomorphism classes of the generated modules.
By \eqref{eq:finite-chain-splitting}, the chain dimensions $r_p(T)$ and
differential ranks $\delta_p(T)$ determine this isomorphism class.
Since $\dim C_p$ bounds both, there are finitely many orbits.

\subsection{Realizable kernels}

Put $\mathcal D=N(X)$. A subcomplex $L\subseteq C$ is
\emph{realizable in $X$} if $L=L(T)$ for some $T\in X_n$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:kernel-realizability}
Let $K$ be a field, $n\geq0$, $C=N(K[\Delta^n])$, and
$X=X(\mathbf c;K)$ with $\mathbf c\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^k$.
For a subcomplex $L\subseteq C$ and $Q=C/L$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $L$ is realizable in $X$.
\item There is a chain monomorphism $Q\hookrightarrow\mathcal D=N(X)$.
\item For every $p\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:realizability-inequalities}
 \dim_K Z_p(Q)\leq\dim_K Z_p(\mathcal D),\qquad
 \dim_K B_p(Q)\leq\dim_K B_p(\mathcal D).
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A realizing map $g_T:C\to\mathcal D$ induces the required monomorphism
$C/L(T)\to\mathcal D$. Any chain monomorphism restricts to injections
on cycles and on boundaries, proving the inequalities.

Conversely, the boundary inequality gives an injection
$B_p(Q)\hookrightarrow B_p(\mathcal D)$ in each degree. By the cycle
inequality, it extends to an injection $j_p:Z_p(Q)\hookrightarrow Z_p(\mathcal D)$.
Choose a complement $W_p$ of $Z_p(Q)$ in $Q_p$. For $p>0$,
$\partial_Q:W_p\to B_{p-1}(Q)$ is an isomorphism. Lift
$j_{p-1}\partial_Q|_{W_p}$ through
$\partial_{\mathcal D}:\mathcal D_p\to B_{p-1}(\mathcal D)$ to a
linear map $\ell_p:W_p\to\mathcal D_p$. Set
$i_p(z+w)=j_p(z)+\ell_p(w)$ and take $i_0=j_0$, since $W_0=0$.
These maps form a chain map. For $p>0$, if $i_p(z+w)=0$, applying
the differential gives $j_{p-1}\partial_Qw=0$, hence $w=0$ and then $z=0$.
The degree-zero map is injective by construction. Thus
$i:Q\to\mathcal D$ is a monomorphism.

The composite $C\to Q\xrightarrow{i}\mathcal D$ corresponds under
Dold--Kan to a simplicial map $P=K[\Delta^n]\to X$. The representing
property of $P$ identifies it with $f_T$ for the image $T$ of the
identity $n$-simplex. Its normalized kernel is $L$, so $L=L(T)$.
\end{proof}

For $p>0$, the cycles and boundaries of $\mathcal D$ coincide and have
dimension $b_p$ by Corollary~\ref{cor:ranks}. In degree zero,
\[
 \dim_K Z_0(\mathcal D)=\prod_a(c_a+1),\qquad
 \dim_K B_0(\mathcal D)=\prod_a(c_a+1)-\prod_a c_a.
\]
Since $B_p(Q)\subseteq Z_p(Q)$, the boundary inequality can add a condition
only in degree zero when every $c_a>0$. For a module associated to a
shape, the cycle inequalities suffice.

For example, when $\mathbf c=(1,1)$, the cycle dimensions
of $\mathcal D$ are $(4,2,0)$ and its boundary dimensions are $(3,2,0)$.
The complex $Q=D^1(K^4)$ is a quotient of $N(K[\Delta^4])$ by
\eqref{eq:simplex-projective}. It satisfies the cycle inequalities,
but $\dim B_0(Q)=4>3=\dim B_0(\mathcal D)$, so it does not embed.

\subsection{Matrix examples and homology in higher degrees}

\begin{example}\label{ex:parameter-jump}
For $\mathbf c=(0,0)$ and $t\in K$, let
\[
 T(t)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\0&1&1\\1&1&t\end{pmatrix}\in X_2,
 \qquad Y_t=\langle T(t)\rangle.
\]
Use the ordered basis $(E_{01},E_{10},E_{11})$ of $N_1(X)$.
Restriction to vertices $1,2$ gives
$y=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\1&t\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ with $d_1y=1$. Subtracting the all-ones matrix
$s_0(1)$ gives $\nu_1y=(t-1)E_{11}$.
The three normalized restrictions are
\[
 v_{01}=(1,0,1),\qquad v_{02}=(0,1,t),\qquad
 v_{12}=(0,0,t-1).
\]
The matrix with these columns has determinant $t-1$. The normalized image of the top simplex
is $E_{12}-E_{21}$, whose differential is $E_{01}-E_{10}$.
The differential $N_1(X)\to K$ sends $(a,b,c)$ to $c$.
Thus the differential ranks of the generated complex are $(0,1,1)$,
indexed by source degree, and
\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
 &(\dim N_0(Y_t),\dim N_1(Y_t),\dim N_2(Y_t))&(\dim H_0(Y_t),\dim H_1(Y_t),\dim H_2(Y_t))\\ \hline
 t\ne1&(1,3,1)&(0,1,0)\\
 t=1&(1,2,1)&(0,0,0).
\end{array}
\]
For $t\ne1$, the class of $E_{01}=v_{01}-(t-1)^{-1}v_{12}$
generates $H_1(Y_t)$.
At $t=1$, the cycle space in degree one is the line spanned by
$v_{01}-v_{02}=E_{01}-E_{10}$, which is already a boundary.

In the vertex basis of $C_0=K^3$, the degree-zero kernel is
\[
 L_0(T(t))=\ker(0,1,t)
   =\operatorname{span}_K\{(1,0,0),(0,-t,1)\}.
\]
These planes vary with $t$. For $t\ne1$, $L_1=L_2=0$, and the quotient
complexes have the same chain dimensions and differential ranks. Hence their kernels
lie in one orbit by Proposition~\ref{prop:quotient-lift}. At $t=1$,
$L_1$ is spanned by $\alpha-s_0d_1\alpha$, where $\alpha:[1]\to[2]$
is the increasing injection with image $\{1,2\}$, and $L_2=0$. For example, $T(4)$ has
first Betti number one over $\mathbb Q$ and zero over $\mathbb F_3$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}\label{ex:sparse-higher-cycle}
Fix $q\geq1$, $k=q+1$ and $\mathbf c\in\mathbb Z_{\geq0}^k$,
and take $T_q=E_{(0,1,\ldots,q)}\in X_q(\mathbf c;K)$.
Every face of $T_q$ is zero, since its index tuple contains $[q]$.
If $\alpha:[p]\to[q]$ misses a vertex, it factors through a coface,
so $X(\alpha)T_q=0$. A surjection $[p]\to[q]$ is the identity for
$p=q$ and gives a degeneracy of $T_q$ for $p>q$. Thus
\[
 N(\langle T_q\rangle)\cong S^q(K),\qquad
 H_q(\langle T_q\rangle)\cong K,\qquad
 H_p(\langle T_q\rangle)=0\quad(p\ne q).
\]
When all $c_a=0$, $T_2$ has one nonzero entry in a $3\times3\times3$
tensor, and $T_3$ has one nonzero entry in shape $(4,4,4,4)$.
In the full module,
\[
 \partial_{q+1}E_{(1,2,\ldots,q+1)}=T_q.
\]
The number of axes is minimal. For $k\leq q$ no index tuple contains
$[q]$, so \eqref{eq:cycles} gives $Z_q(N(X))=0$. Every simplicial
submodule of such an $X$ therefore has zero $q$th homology.
\end{example}

The script \path{experiments/kernel_sequences.py} computes kernels,
quotients and Betti numbers for $T(t)$ at integer values of $t$ and
for $T_q$ with $\mathbf c=0$.

\subsection{The incidence complex of a graph}

Let $A$ be a nonzero commutative ring with identity.
Number the vertices of a finite graph $G$ from $1$ to $v$ and its edges from
$1$ to $e$. Loops and parallel edges are allowed. Orient edge $j$ from
$u_j$ to $w_j$.
Take $X=X((r,r,r);A)$ with $r\geq\max(v,e)$. Assign to each
vertex $t$ and each edge $j$ the tensors
\[
 a_t=E_{(0,t,t)}\in X_0,\qquad
 T_j=E_{(0,u_j+1,u_j+1)}+E_{(1,w_j+1,w_j+1)}
              +E_{(0,1,j+1)}\in X_1.
\]
Deleting index $1$ gives $d_1T_j=a_{u_j}$, and deleting index $0$ gives
$d_0T_j=a_{w_j}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:graph}
The simplicial submodule $Y_G$ generated by the $a_t$ and $T_j$
has normalized complex $A^e\to A^v$ in degrees $1,0$, with
differential sending edge $j$ to its target minus its source.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The vertex tensors are distinct standard basis vectors of $X_0$. The normalized
edges $b_j=T_j-s_0a_{u_j}$ are linearly independent because the
coordinate $(0,1,j+1)$ occurs with coefficient $1$ in $b_j$ and
vanishes on the other normalized edges. Also
$d_0b_j=a_{w_j}-a_{u_j}$. The other degree-one generators are
degeneracies of vertices, and every generator in higher degrees
is a degeneracy. The normalization projection kills these terms.
\end{proof}

On each component, the boundaries of the edges of a spanning tree form
a basis of the kernel of the augmentation $a_t\mapsto 1$.
From each remaining edge subtract the signed sum of the edges on the tree
path from its source to its target, with signs given by the orientations.
These differences form a basis of the cycle module.
Thus $H_0(Y_G)\cong A^b$ and $H_1(Y_G)\cong A^{e-v+b}$, where $b$
is the number of components. The Petersen graph has ten vertices,
fifteen edges, and one component, so its first homology is $A^6$.
The script \path{rank_shifted_homology.py} computes its incidence
rank from the normalized tensor faces over $\mathbb Q$
\cite{SparseShiftedTensors}.

For a two-axis module $X'=X((c_1,c_2);A)$, the cycles in $N_1(X')$ have basis $E_{(0,1)}$ and
$E_{(1,0)}$ by \eqref{eq:cycles}. Over a field, every simplicial
submodule of $X'$ therefore has first Betti number at most two.

\subsection{Boundary ranks of ordered adjacency matrices}

Let $H$ be a simple graph on $n\geq2$ vertices. For each order
$\pi=(v_0,\ldots,v_{n-1})$, insert one isolated vertex between successive
vertices. Write $B_\pi$ for the simplicial boundary of the resulting
$(2n-1)\times(2n-1)$ adjacency matrix over $\mathbb F_2$.
Figure~\ref{fig:interleaved-path} shows the construction for a path on three vertices.
Let $N_\pi$ be the adjacency matrix of $H$ with each edge oriented forward
in the order $\pi$.
Let $p(H)$ be the least number of vertex-disjoint paths covering $V(H)$.
A path may have chords or consist of a single vertex.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:ordered-graph-ranks}
For every order $\pi$ of a finite simple graph $H$ on $n\geq2$ vertices,
$\rank B_\pi=2\rank N_\pi$ over $\mathbb F_2$.
The maximum of $\rank B_\pi$ over all orders is $2(n-p(H))$.
An order gives rank $2n-2$ precisely when it traces a Hamiltonian path.
Thus the multiplicity of full rank among the $n!$ orders counts Hamiltonian
paths, with each path and its reverse counted separately.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $N=N_\pi$. Deleting either endpoint removes the edge $v_iv_j$.
For $i<j$, there are $2i$ deletions before both endpoints,
$2(j-i)-1$ between them, and $2(n-1-j)$ after both. Thus only the
entry at $(2i,2j-1)$ and its transpose survive in the boundary.
Grouping even indices before odd ones gives off-diagonal blocks $U,U^\top$
and zero diagonal blocks, where $U$ is $N$ without its zero first column
and zero last row. Hence $\rank B_\pi=2\rank N$. Since $U$ is upper
triangular, $\det B_\pi=(\det U)^2=\prod_{i=0}^{n-2}N_{i,i+1}$ in
$\mathbb F_2$. This product is $1$ precisely when consecutive vertices
of $\pi$ are adjacent.

A nonzero minor of $N$ of order $r$ has a determinant term selecting $r$
edges with distinct initial vertices and distinct terminal vertices. Since all edges point forward,
they give a cover by $n-r$ paths, including singletons. Conversely,
concatenate the paths of a minimum cover, listing each in traversal order.
Their $n-p(H)$ edges join consecutive vertices in this order. Taking
their initial vertices as rows and their terminal vertices as columns, in
order, gives an upper triangular submatrix with diagonal entries $1$.
\end{proof}

Let $J$ be the all-ones matrix. Over $\mathbb Z$, Stanley's formula
identifies the coefficient of $t^j$ in $\det(I+t(J-N_\pi))$ with the
number of directed paths on $j$ vertices, $1\leq j\leq n$
\cite{StanleyPaths}. For $j=n$ only $\pi$ can be such a path, so
$\det(J-N_\pi)\bmod 2=\det B_\pi$. Subtracting the last row of $J-N_\pi$
from each earlier row gives $\rank(J-N_\pi)=1+\rank N_\pi$ over
$\mathbb F_2$, since the first column of $N_\pi$ is zero.

For the $4\times4$ rook graph, counting full-rank mod-$2$ boundaries
recovers the number of oriented Hamiltonian paths. Let $a_{uv}$ be $1$
if $u$ and $v$ are adjacent and $0$ otherwise. For each vertex order
$\pi=(v_0,\ldots,v_{15})$, the product below is the integer $0$ or $1$
represented by $\det_{\mathbb F_2}B_\pi$. Summing over all orders gives
\[
 \#\{\pi:\rank_{\mathbb F_2}B_\pi=30\}
 =\sum_\pi\prod_{i=0}^{14}a_{v_i v_{i+1}}=22{,}394{,}880.
\]
This agrees with the fourth term of OEIS A096970 \cite{OEISRook},
which counts each path and its reverse separately. The script
\path{interleaved_graph_paths.py} evaluates the sum by grouping paths
by endpoint and set of unused vertices.

The Shrikhande graph has the same strongly regular parameters $(16,6,2,2)$
and adjacency spectrum $6^1,2^6,(-2)^9$ as the rook graph
\cite{Shrikhande1959}, but rank~$30$ occurs in $22{,}230{,}528$ of its
orders. Thus the adjacency spectrum does not determine the distribution
of $\rank B_\pi$. For the Petersen graph, rank~$18$ occurs in $240$ orders.

Without the inserted isolated vertices, the distribution of boundary ranks does not determine the
Hamiltonian path count. Every order gives the zero boundary for both $K_4$
and the edgeless graph on four vertices, since the four deletion faces of
$K_4$ coincide and cancel over $\mathbb F_2$. The two graphs have $24$ and
$0$ oriented Hamiltonian paths, respectively.

\section{Kernel families in a product of Grassmannians}
\label{app:kernel-families}
Fix a field $K$, a degree $n$ and a tuple $\mathbf c$ as in Section~\ref{sec:generated}.
As $T$ varies in the affine space $W=X_n(\mathbf c;K)$, the kernels
$L_p(T)=\ker(g_{T,p}:C_p\to N_p(X))$ form families of subspaces of $C_p$.

For a finite-dimensional $K$-vector space $V$ and $0\leq r\leq\dim V$,
write $\operatorname{Gr}(r,V)$ for the Grassmannian of $r$-dimensional
subspaces of $V$. Its points over an extension $F/K$ correspond to the
$r$-planes in $V\otimes_K F$. The fiber of its tautological subbundle
at such a point is the corresponding plane.

For $0\leq p\leq n$, let $r_p$ be the rank of $g_{T,p}$ over the
function field $K(W)$ and put $\ell_p=\dim C_p-r_p$.
Let $U\subseteq W$ be the locus where, for each $p$, some minor of order
$r_p$ is nonzero. This open set contains the generic point of $W$.
The kernels on $U$ define a morphism
\begin{equation}\label{eq:kernel-map}
 \Psi:U\longrightarrow G=\prod_{p=0}^n\operatorname{Gr}(\ell_p,C_p),
 \qquad T\longmapsto(L_p(T))_p.
\end{equation}
Each $L(T)$ is a subcomplex since $g_T$ is a chain map.
The image $\mathcal M=\Psi(U)$ in the underlying space of $G$ is
constructible by Chevalley's theorem \cite[Tag 054K]{Stacks}.
Let $Y$ be its reduced Zariski closure.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:kernel-rationality}
If $K$ is infinite, the reduced closure $Y$ of the kernel family
$\Psi(U)$ is birational over $K$ to an affine space.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Since $U$ is irreducible, $Y$ is integral. In $W\times Y$ let $\mathcal E$
be the incidence scheme defined by $g_{T,p}(L_p)=0$ on the tautological
subbundles. These equations are linear in $T$. Over $U$ the inclusion
$L_p\subseteq\ker g_{T,p}$ is an equality because both are subbundles of the
same trivial bundle, of rank $\ell_p$. Thus $\mathcal E\cap(U\times Y)$
is the graph of $\Psi$.

Choose bases of the tautological subbundles near the generic point of $Y$.
In the matrix of the incidence equations choose a nonzero minor of maximal
order $m$. Let $\pi:W\to\mathbb A_K^d$ project onto the $d=\dim W-m$
coordinates outside its columns. On the nonempty open $Y^\circ$ where
this minor is invertible, solving for the other $m$ coordinates identifies
$\mathcal E|_{Y^\circ}$ with $Y^\circ\times\mathbb A_K^d$.
The graph is open in $\mathcal E$, so $(\Psi,\pi)$ identifies
$\Psi^{-1}(Y^\circ)$ with an open subset of this product.

The nonempty open $\Psi^{-1}(Y^\circ)\subseteq W$ contains a $K$-point
$T_0$ because $K$ is infinite. Put $c=\pi(T_0)$ and $S=\pi^{-1}(c)$.
Restricting the isomorphism to $\pi=c$ identifies the nonempty open
$S\cap\Psi^{-1}(Y^\circ)$ in $S$ with an open subset of $Y^\circ$.
Since $S\cong\mathbb A_K^{\dim W-d}$, this proves the assertion.
\end{proof}

Identify $\operatorname{Gr}(2,K^3)$ with the dual projective plane by sending
the plane $\ker(a,b,c)$ to the point $[a:b:c]$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:three-by-three}
For shape $(3,3)$ over any field $K$, the kernel dimensions on $U$ in
degrees $0,1,2$ are $2,0,0$. The image of $\Psi$ is
\[
 \mathcal M=\mathbb P_K^2\setminus\{[1:1:1]\}.
\]
Its $K$-points form one orbit under chain automorphisms of $C$.
For each $T\in U(K)$ the generated submodule has homology
\[
 H_1(\langle T\rangle)\cong K,
 \qquad H_j(\langle T\rangle)=0\quad(j\ne1).
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Write $T=(t_{ij})$ and $(a,b,c)=(t_{00},t_{11},t_{22})$.
In degree zero $g_{T,0}$ is the row $(a,b,c):K^3\to K$.
In degree one, normalization subtracts $t_{ii}$ from every entry of
the restriction to $i,j$, for $i<j$. In the basis $(E_{01},E_{10},E_{11})$
of $N_1(X)$ the normalized restrictions to $01,02,12$ are the columns of
\[
 B_T=\begin{pmatrix}
 t_{01}-a&t_{02}-a&t_{12}-b\\
 t_{10}-a&t_{20}-a&t_{21}-b\\
 b-a&c-a&c-b
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
For fixed $a,b,c$ the first two rows are arbitrary. Over any extension
of $K$ they can complete the last row to a basis exactly when $a,b,c$
are not all equal. If $\det B_T\ne0$, then $g_{T,0}$ has rank one
and $g_{T,1}$ is injective. Since the top simplex of $\Delta^2$ has
nonzero boundary and spans $C_2$, the chain-map identity makes $g_{T,2}$ injective.
Thus the maximal ranks are $(1,3,1)$ and $U=\{T:\det B_T\ne0\}$.
The kernel tuple on $U$ is $(\ker(a,b,c),0,0)$. The choice of rows over
each residue field gives the stated image. Above $[a:b:c]\in\mathcal M$
the fiber is the open subset $\det B_T\ne0$ of the seven-dimensional
linear space of matrices whose diagonal lies on the line spanned by $(a,b,c)$.

The restriction of the augmentation $(1,1,1)$ to $\ker(a,b,c)$
is surjective with one-dimensional kernel, since $(a,b,c)$ is
nonconstant. Formula \eqref{eq:generated-homology} gives the homology.
The quotient complexes have the same chain dimensions and differential
ranks, so \eqref{eq:finite-chain-splitting} makes them isomorphic.
Proposition~\ref{prop:quotient-lift} gives the orbit assertion.
\end{proof}

The plane $\ker(1,1,1)$ does occur in degree zero outside $U$.
For the all-ones matrix the kernel tuple is $(\ker(1,1,1),C_1,C_2)$.
This matrix generates the constant simplicial module $K$, with homology
$K$ in degree zero alone.
