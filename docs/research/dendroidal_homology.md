# Dendroidal homology for edge-indexed arrays

The [rooted-tree construction](../../tex/dendroidal_section.tex) associates arrays to the colours of a tree. Two homological constructions apply to different aspects of this presheaf: planar dendroidal normalization retains its additive structure, whereas dendroidal-set homology starts from its underlying sets.

## The array presheaf

Let $A$ be a commutative ring, let $k\geq1$, and fix finite sets $C_1,\ldots,C_k$. For a rooted tree $T$ with edge set $E(T)$, put
$$
I(T)=\prod_{a=1}^k(E(T)\sqcup C_a),\qquad D(T)=A^{I(T)}.
$$
A morphism $f:S\to T$ in the symmetric dendroidal category $\Omega$ induces a colour map $E(S)\to E(T)$. Extend this map by the identity on each $C_a$ and take products to obtain $I(f):I(S)\to I(T)$. The restriction map is
$$
f^*:D(T)\longrightarrow D(S),\qquad f^*(x)=x\circ I(f).
$$
Composition of colour maps gives a presheaf $D:\Omega^{\mathrm{op}}\to\mathbf{Mod}_A$. On a linear tree $L_n$, there are $n+1$ edges, and the restriction to linear trees is the simplicial array module with shape $(n+1+|C_1|,\ldots,n+1+|C_k|)$.

## Planar normalization

Let $u:\Omega_p\to\Omega$ forget the ordering of inputs at each vertex of a planar rooted tree. Restriction gives the planar dendroidal abelian group $u^*D$, with its inherited $A$-module structure.

Gutiérrez, Lukacs, and Weiss construct an equivalence between planar dendroidal abelian groups and planar dendroidal complexes. The latter are indexed by trees and have face operators satisfying anticommutative elementary-face relations. On each maximal linear part, the faces except the last in the induced order are called normal (Definition 5.1). Their normalized component is
$$
N(u^*D)_T=\bigcap_{\delta:S\to T\ \mathrm{normal}}\ker(\delta^*).
$$
The Moore face operators include signed sums along these linear parts. This is a tree-indexed normalization; restriction to linear trees gives classical Dold–Kan normalization, with the endpoint convention chosen consistently. See [§§5–7 and Theorem 7.3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0909.3995).

A binary corolla has no unary linear part and hence no normal faces. Therefore $N(u^*D)_{C_2}=D(C_2)$. With two axes and empty tails this component has rank nine. In contrast, the intersection of the kernels of all three colour faces consists of arrays with zero diagonal and has rank six. This latter submodule is the boundary kernel used for tree horns.

## Homology of the underlying dendroidal set

Write $UD$ for the set-valued presheaf underlying $D$. Bašić and Nikolaus form signed chains generated by triples $(T,p,x)$, where $p$ is a planar structure and $x\in UD(T)$. The boundary sums elementary faces; signed isomorphism relations identify different representatives, and quotienting degenerate generators gives normalized chains. A dendroidal set is normal when the automorphism group of each tree acts freely on its dendrices of that shape. Choose a normal cofibrant replacement $\widetilde{UD}\to UD$ in the stable model structure. With an abelian coefficient group $B$, their homology is
$$
H_n^{\mathrm{BN}}(UD;B)=H_n\bigl(\operatorname{Ch}^{\mathrm{un}}(\widetilde{UD})\otimes_{\mathbb Z}B\bigr).
$$
See [§4 and Definitions 2.5 and 5.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.00702).

This construction first takes free abelian groups on set elements. Thus the generators $[x+y]$, $[x]$, and $[y]$ carry no additive relation inherited from the array module. With one axis, empty tails, and $A=\mathbb Q$, the module $D(\eta)=\mathbb Q$ has rank one, while $\mathbb Z[\mathbb Q]$ has infinitely many basis generators.

The input interchange of the binary corolla fixes the zero array in $D(C_2)$. Hence $UD$ is not normal. A signed complex formed directly from array values would require a separate definition and comparison theorem to identify its homology with $H_*^{\mathrm{BN}}(UD;B)$.

## Finite representable examples

For a finite tree $T$, the normalized chains of the representable $\Omega[T]$ give finite matrices. These chains describe operadic maps into $T$, rather than arrays in $D(T)$.

For the binary corolla with root $r$ and leaves $a,b$, the chain groups over a field $\mathbb F$ have bases $(r,a,b)$ in degree zero and the corolla in degree one. The boundary column is $(-1,1,1)^{\mathsf T}$, so the Betti numbers are $(2,0)$. Quotienting by its boundary leaves one generator in degree one. These results agree with [Corollaries 5.12–5.13](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.00702).

The two-vertex tree with operations $u:a\to e$ and $v:(e,b)\to r$ also has the composite $w:(a,b)\to r$. With bases $(a,e,b,r)$, $(u,v,w)$, and $(T)$, respectively, its normalized boundaries are
$$
B_1=\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&1\\
-1&1&0\\
0&1&1\\
0&-1&-1
\end{pmatrix},\qquad
B_2=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The three terms in $B_2$ are the root pruning, top pruning, and inner contraction, with the signs of Definition 3.1 of Bašić–Nikolaus. Thus $B_1B_2=0$. The columns for $u$ and $v$ are independent and the column for $w$ is their sum. The boundary ranks are therefore two and one over every field, giving Betti numbers $(2,0,0)$. The relative boundary quotient is concentrated in degree two with dimension one.

The boundary subpresheaf of the two-vertex tree retains the four colours
and three one-vertex operations, with differential $B_1$ above. Its Betti
numbers are $(2,1)$, and $u+v-w$ represents its first homology class.
The connecting homomorphism sends the relative class of $T$ to this
boundary class, so that relative class has no absolute cycle lift.

## Tree data needed for computation

Tree shape determines the degree and the available faces. A binary corolla and $L_2$ both have three edges, but they have one and two vertices. Their arrays can both have shape $3\times3$, although their dendroidal degrees and face maps differ. The simultaneous-deletion boundary in the tensor library computes the simplicial restriction.

General tree calculations require explicit inner and outer faces, unary degeneracies, and the appropriate planar signs or signed automorphism relations. A finite collection of trees must contain the faces used by its differential. A bound on the number of vertices alone permits infinitely many corollas of different arities. The face collections of fixed finite trees supply finite examples of the form above.
